From Product Owner to Engineer — what I gained by crossing the line

Most engineers never sit in a PO seat. I did both — by choice. Here's what changes when you understand both sides.

⏱ 6 min read

Most engineers I've worked with have never written a user story from scratch. Never had to explain to a stakeholder why a feature is being delayed. Never sat in a room where the conversation isn't about code at all — it's about customers, revenue, and decisions that will take months to implement but seconds to regret.

I have. And I chose to.

Before I became a full-time Software Engineer, I spent years as a Product Owner and IT Operations Manager. It wasn't a detour. It was, in hindsight, the best technical education I could have had — even though I wasn't writing a single line of code.

"The best engineers I've met aren't the ones who write the most elegant code. They're the ones who understand why the code needs to exist."

Why I made the switch — deliberately

The decision to move into engineering was planned. I didn't fall into it. I had been working closely with development teams for years, defining requirements, reviewing deliverables, and constantly asking myself: "If I knew how to build this, would I have designed it differently?"

The answer was almost always yes.

I saw features built exactly as specified — and still fail to solve the problem. I saw engineers deliver perfectly functional code that nobody used. I saw the gap between what product asked for and what users actually needed, and I realised that gap often came from the fact that the people building the thing didn't fully understand the business context around it.

I wanted to be on the other side of that table. But I wanted to bring everything I'd learned with me.

What actually changed when I crossed the line

I read tickets differently

Most engineers read a ticket and ask: "How do I implement this?" After years as a PO, my first question is always: "Why does this exist?" Not to be difficult — but because the answer almost always changes how I implement it, or whether I implement it exactly as written at all.

A ticket is a proposed solution to a problem. The problem is what matters. And sometimes the proposed solution isn't the best one.1

I understand what "done" actually means

In engineering, "done" usually means the code works, tests pass, and it's deployed. In product, "done" means users can do something they couldn't do before — and the business moves in the right direction because of it.

These are very different definitions. Holding both in your head at the same time makes you a fundamentally different kind of engineer.

I communicate across the table, not just across the codebase

I've been in rooms where engineers and product managers are speaking completely different languages. The engineer says "we need to refactor the service layer before we can add this feature." The PM hears "no, not now." Neither is wrong — but the conversation breaks down.

Having sat on both sides, I know how to translate. I know what a PM actually needs to hear to make a good decision, and I know how to advocate for technical concerns in a way that lands.

The real gain: understanding impact

If I had to name the single biggest thing my PO experience gave me as an engineer, it's this: I understand the real-world impact of what I build.

Not in a vague, abstract way. Concretely. I know that a slow API doesn't just fail a performance benchmark — it increases cart abandonment. I know that a poorly designed onboarding flow doesn't just score badly in a UX review — it costs the business customers every single day it stays broken. I know that technical debt isn't just a code quality issue — it's a speed issue, and speed is a business issue.

That knowledge changes how I prioritise. It changes how I push back. It changes what I choose to care about.

"Code is easy to write. Knowing what to write — and why — is the hard part."

What I'd tell engineers who've never been a PO

You don't need to switch roles to develop this perspective. But you do need to actively seek it out. Join product planning sessions even when you're not required to. Ask your PM what success looks like for the feature you're building. Read the analytics after you ship something. Talk to support about what users actually complain about.

The gap between a developer and a great engineer is often not technical. It's contextual. And context is everywhere — if you decide to look for it.
